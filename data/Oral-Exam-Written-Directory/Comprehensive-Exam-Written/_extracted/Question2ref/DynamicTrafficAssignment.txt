1. Introduction
Traffic events (vehicle accidents, road closures, demand peaks, etc.) happen on a daily basis on road networks. Most typi-
cal effects are related to local capacity reductions, or global flow increases, which may both cause oversaturation, congestion
and then delays. Rerouting takes place when drivers, while travelling on the network, realize that the situation is atypical,
and then decide en-trip to change their path, so as to partially avoid the negative consequences of the event.
Forecasting how events affect the traffic pattern is of particular importance, since proper monitoring (to assess impacts)
and management (to take actions) rely on predictions. Due to events, both travel times and traffic flows change with respect
to typical values, not only for their direct effects, but also as a consequence of their indirect effects. More specifically, the
event impacts may propagate on the network both upstream because of queue spillback and sideways because of rerouting,
causing further delays ( Li et al., 2015 ). Modelling these effects on the supply side is relatively straightforward; if the route

It is instead much more challenging to represent the effects on the demand side, namely how drivers are first informed and
can then react to unexpected events by changing en-trip their pre-trip route choice.
Some events are objectively unpredictable, and some other are only subjectively such. In both cases, not all drivers are
informed in due time, i.e. before starting their trip. Moreover, drivers tend to be reluctant in changing their habitual path,
which they clearly know better than currently optimal alternatives, so that modest delays are typically accepted without
rerouting. Deficient information and prudential behaviour contradict the main assumptions of deterministic user equilibrium,
i.e. perfect information and rational behaviour. Moreover, stochastic assignment is not of great help in reproducing the
situation under analysis, because events produce perturbations of equilibrium which have an anisotropic gradient in space
and time. Although several rerouting models were proposed in the literature, none of them allows at the same time to
represent drivers’ behaviour realistically and to simulate efficiently how traffic flows and travel times evolve due to the
event on large networks.
Fig. 1 illustrates the rerouting phenomenon in an urban context. The path chosen pre-trip by the driver, that is optimal in
terms of its typical costs, is not optimal anymore due to the unexpected event and to the delay that it caused. However, the
driver may decide to change his/her path, to one that is optimal in terms of actual costs, only after receiving information
about the traffic event and then realizing that he/she can partially avoid its negative consequences.
Other authors call this phenomenon adaptation ( Gao et al., 2010 ), recourse ( Unnikrishnan and Waller, 2009 ) or en-route
switching decisions ( Hu and Mahmassani, 1997 ); if path changing is limited to pre-trip choices, it is usually referred to as
route-swapping and handled in the context of day-to-day models ( Cantarella and Watling, 2016 ). The relevance of rerout-
ing has raised in the last years as the number of informed drivers has substantially increased with the growing market
penetration of Advanced Traveller Information Systems (ATIS).
In this paper, we present the Information Comply Model (ICM), which reproduces rerouting as a two-stage process,
where drivers, first become aware of the event with a certain probability at each arc, thanks to a given ATIS configuration
of available information sources (radio, VMS, internet apps, etc.), and second decide whether to change to an optimal path
with a certain probability at each node, based on expected gains and avoided losses. Finally, rerouting drivers use their
actual prediction of travel times and costs to choose their new path to destination. This schema results in a particular
fixed-point formulation of Dynamic Traffic Assignment (DTA) where rerouting decisions are iteratively adjusted to reach an
equilibrium which incorporates the above two-stage process.
We apply the ICM in the macroscopic DTA framework of Gentile (2016) , which is here properly extended to represent
rerouting. The resulting model has also been implemented as an add-in of PTV Optima, a commercial software solution

1.1. Background
Thanks to recent developments, DTA is now applied not only in off-line transportation planning, but also in real-
time traffic management (e.g. PTV Optima, Gentile and Meschini, 2011 ; DynaMIT 2.0, Lu et al., 2015 ; AIMSUN online,
Frisoet al., 2013 ). This, however, raises a number of issues, one of which is addressed in this paper: as Peeta and Mahmas-
sani (1995) and later Dobler et al. (2012) pointed out, the usual equilibrium models, even if based on dynamic assignment
methods, fail to reproduce atypical and unexpected situations where drivers reroute trying to avoid a traffic event.
The need for improvement is motivated by the fact that the state-of-the-practice presents two solutions to handle unex-
pected events within DTA, both of them intrinsically wrong. The first one is to solve a Dynamic User Equilibrium problem,
assuming that the event and its consequences on travel times and traffic flows are perfectly known to drivers, which results
in a predictive behaviour where users reroute before the event took place. The second one is to solve a Dynamic Network
Loading problem, assuming that drivers will stick to their habitual path without rerouting, which results in the propagation
of the demand flows on the network where the impacts of the event are taken into account only on the supply side. This is
why an intermediate, more realistic model is looked-for and proposed in this paper.
The rerouting problem can be derived from the route choice problem (for a broad review of route choice models we refer
to Prato, 2009 ). Initial formulations, where the arc costs are assumed fixed and deterministic, have been gradually improved
and became applicable also to the case of en-trip rerouting. Polychronopoulos and Tsitsiklis (1993) address the shortest path
problem on graphs with random arc costs which provides a so-called policy that leads from an origin to a destination node
with minimal expected cost. Gao and Chabini (2006) further extend this concept into the optimal routing policy problem
on dynamic stochastic networks, where link travel times are modelled as random variables with time-dependent distribu-
tions. Another valuable concept is the hybrid route choice model, first introduced by Mahmassani and Jayakrishnan (1991) ,
where users can adapt their path while travelling. Waller and Ziliaskopoulos (2002) utilize this concept to introduce the
online shortest path problem, where decision makers have the opportunity to reevaluate their remaining path en-trip when
information becomes available.
Coupling en-trip path adaptation and stochastic arc costs leads to the concept of adaptive routing policy , defined as a
decision rule that maps all possible revealed traffic conditions to the next link choices, subject to the available informa-
tion ( Gao et al., 2008 ). This allows to reproduce travellers’ strategic behaviour when adapting to revealed traffic conditions
en route. The random path utilities are sequentially updated at each node with the revealed traffic conditions and ap-
plied to revise the route choices. It is assumed that travellers know the probabilistic distributions of link travel times and
the correlations among them. The computational burden of this assumption may somehow be reduced by: (1) an approx-
imate heuristics ( Gao and Chabini, 2006 ); (2) simplifying the unknown travel time distributions with incomplete statistics
( Flajolet et al., 2017 ); or (3) sampling them from simulation results ( Snowdon et al., 2012 ). Valuably, the adaptive routing
policy can be further extended to the Prospect Theory ( Gao et al., 2010 ), which allows to reproduce the twofold attitudes
toward travel time variability: risk seeking under high probability and risk averse under low probability; that cannot be
captured when the utility is assumed linear. Unnikrishnan and Waller (2009) proposed an equilibrium framework defined
on routing policies instead of paths: the network is in equilibrium if no user can improve his/her expected cost by changing
the routing policies (i.e. each user chooses a routing policy with minimum expected cost).
In contrast to the above, Hu and Mahmassani (1997) assume that users have access to full knowledge about the actual
network costs. Perfectly informed users, however, behave with bounded rationality, so that rerouting takes place only if the
difference between actual and typical travel times is greater than a given indifference threshold. In the model proposed by
Mahmassani and Jayakrishnan (1991) drivers update their estimation of travel costs while travelling. The model was further
extended through a rolling-horizon approach by Peeta and Mahmassani (1995) , so that a given share of drivers periodically
becomes aware about the actual travel costs and can update en-trip their path choice.
A similar hybrid model by Pel et al. (2009) requires path enumeration and is thus limited to a small networks, yet pro-
vides concepts which we adopt in this paper to model rerouting, namely: (1) the rerouting utility shall be computed subject
to the destination; (2) drivers may perceive the received information in different ways and inaccurately. Corthout et al.
(2011) complements this by stating that: (3) informed drivers can anticipate downstream congestion, and (4) the share of
rerouting drivers is related to the travel cost gains from the current decision point to the destination. The other concepts
founding our approach to rerouting are: (5) a specific version of multi-class assignment where informed and uninformed
drivers are propagated jointly but utilizing different cost patterns (as proposed in Mahmassani and Jayakrishnan, 1991 ); (6)
representing the awareness process as a transition of unaware drivers into aware drivers at intermediate nodes ( Ma et al.,
2016 ).

Another, lately popular approach ( Dobler et al., 2012; Xiong et al., 2016; Ma et al., 2016; Cao et al., 2016 ) is to repro-
duce rerouting with agent-based microscopic simulators, which provide a flexible framework to model driver acquisition of
information and path choice behaviour in detail, yet are by nature stochastic and complex.
The adaptive behaviour of drivers is broadly investigated in the day-to-day context. In particular, Cantarella and
Cascetta (1995) define the probability of choosing a path in a given day as the product of: (a) the probability of recon-
sidering the choice done in the previous day and (b) the conditional probability of choosing that path given that the choice
of the previous day is reconsidered. Watling and Hazelton (2003) simulate the learning process through an exponential
smoothing: cyclically a share of users update the expected travel times with the actual experience. Han et al. (2011) analyse
the impact of ATIS on day-to-day adaptation. This is further examined by Bifulco et al. (2016) , showing that an inaccurate
real-time information can destabilize the day-to-day dynamic system, and ATIS route indications are most efficient when the
information is accurate. The day-to-day dynamic assignment is formulated by Bifulco et al. (2016) as a fixed point problem
with predicted travel costs converging to the actual travel costs. In this paper we propose a similar fixed-point problem, but
the DTA model is solved in the within-day context.
An excellent review of recent findings about the effects of information on travellers’ behaviour can be found in Ben-
Elia and Avineri (2015) . The revealed behaviour is complex and includes several phenomena like reinforced learning, risk
and loss aversion, probability weighting, anchoring and ambiguity aversion, regret aversion. These behavioural theories are
supported by experimental findings. Selected results from Stated Preference experiments, where travellers choose a route
in a specific information context, are: (1) prescriptive information, where drivers are explicitly informed with the optimal
route, has the largest impact on behaviour ( Ben-Elia et al., 2013 ); (2) real-time information availability at downstream nodes
encourages participants to less risk-averse behaviour ( Lu et al., 2010 ); (3) more detailed VMS information yield higher di-
version rates (Peeta at al., 20 0 0) ; (4) drivers’ reaction is both heterogenic and stochastic ( Dell’Orco and Marinelli, 2017 );
(5) providing information on the range of travel times, i.e. the difference between maximum and minimum, can trigger
risky behaviour ( Katsikopoulos et al., 2002 ). In the typical recent Revealed Preference experiments travellers are traced with
GPS ( Papinski et al., 2009; Schlaich, 2010; Vacca and Meloni, 2015 ). Ramos (2015) applied the sequential logit model of
Fosgerau et al. (2013) on GPS traces and revealed that drivers: (1) are more sensitive to actual travel times when they are
informed; (2) comply with pre-trip, rather than en-trip, information, but (3) consult travel information just before departing;
(4) make distinctions among information sources which then impact differently on their behaviour.
The researches on Twitter data (e.g. Lerman and Ghosh, 2010; Sakaki et al., 2010; Bakshy et al., 2012 ) provide valuable
insights on the information spread process. Its dynamics and range have been observed through the tweets posted after
relevant emergencies: earthquake, hurricane, traffic events, rebellions, etc. Procter et al. (2013) show how information prop-
agated through the Twitter network in time and obtained a Rayleigh-like shape of the spread curve, in-line with evacuation
models ( Lindell and Prater, 2007 ). Ghosh and Lerman (2014) show that information in the communication network is either
completely negligible or significant and spreads like a virus, being reposted forward with exponential probability.
1.2. Objectives and contributions
Although the rerouting phenomenon was the object for many researches, it can be still considered an open issue, since
the applicability of the proposed models is limited, due to excessive simplification or complexity.
Our contribution is a workable model that can be effectively applied on real-size networks to realistically represent the
joint effects on supply (congestion) and demand (rerouting) of a single traffic event in presence of multiple information
sources. To this end we extend the framework for macroscopic within-day DTA presented in Gentile (2016) .
The methodology presented in this paper is built mainly on our previous research on DTA ( Bellei et al., 2005; Bellei
et al., 2006; Gentile et al., 2007; Gentile, 2015 ), which exploits implicit path enumeration through a sequential approach to
route choice ( Gentile and Papola, 2006; Long et al., 2013 ) and the Link Transmission Model ( Yperman, 2007; Gentile, 2010;
Nezamuddin and Boyles, 2015 ) as a simulation engine on the supply side.
After pointing out the limitations of the classic DTA to represent rerouting, we identify a suitable way to include the
phenomenon in the model structure. Specifically, we based the proposed formulation on implicit path enumeration, as this
allows to reproduce local en-trip decisions at nodes for users directed toward a given destination and to avoid the compu-
tation of shortest paths.
We adopted temporal layers to represent dynamics as this easies the flow handling when travel times change, which is
instead challenging in trajectory based algorithms. Specifically, the path choices of the reference equilibrium can be used
in the event scenario. Moreover, temporal layers allow for a less dense time discretization with long time intervals (e.g.
5–10 min), which is crucial for real-time applications and large networks.
Then, we present the ICM, which is suited for the specific DTA framework and reproduces rerouting as a two-stage
process: first, drivers become aware about the event and its consequences on traffic; second, drivers may decide to change
path.
We represent the probability of becoming aware of the event as the joint probability of being informed by various ATIS
sources (radio, VMS, mobile apps), not only as a function of their penetration rate, but also of the space and time coordinates
of the driver wrt those of the event, as in Kucharski and Gentile (2015) .
We represent the probability of rerouting by aware drivers, who are reluctant to change, through a random utility model,
which takes into account expected gains and avoided losses.

This way we are capable of representing the evolution of the rerouting phenomena in time and space when the infor-
mation about the event and its consequences on traffic spread onto the network.
Since the outcomes of rerouting decisions are dependent on rerouting decisions made by other drivers, providing a sound
equilibrium model is non-trivial. To this end we propose a fixed-point framework which converges to a solution where
rerouting drivers will not only reduce their costs, but also their experienced travel costs will be in line with the expected
ones.
Finally, ICM is the first (up to authors knowledge) macroscopic DTA model capable of simulating a scenario with one
traffic event on large-size networks in a reasonable computing time (comparable to dynamic user equilibrium), while rep-
resenting the effect of multiple information sources and stochastic rerouting decisions.
2. A macroscopic Dynamic Traffic Assignment model to road networks
2.1. Schematic formulation of the DTA model
The road network is here represented through a directed graph ( N, A ), where N is the set of nodes and A ⊆ N ×N is the
set of arcs. Each arc a ∈ A has a (possibly time varying) vector of supply characteristics s a ( τ ), e.g. length, free flow speed,
capacity, jam density, signal settings, etc., that allow to represent its performances under different traffic conditions. The
initial node of the generic arc a ∈ A , referred to as tail , is denoted N a –∈ N , while the final node, referred to as head , is denoted
N a + ∈ N . The set of arcs exiting the generic node i ∈ N , referred to as forward star , is denoted A i + = { a ∈ A: N a –= i }; the set of
arcs entering node i ∈ N , referred to as backward star , is denoted A i –= { a ∈ A: N a + = i }.

Below, we describe in a compact form the DTA model to which the proposed ICM was applied; for more details we refer
to Gentile (2016) .
Dynamic Traffic Assignment can be regarded as the system of two sub-models: supply , being a function of the demand,
and demand , being a function of the supply.
The supply model yields the network performance pattern in terms of travel times t corresponding to the network usage
pattern in terms of destination flows q for given supply characteristics s :
t = Supply ( q ;s ). (1)
The proposed DTA considers a macroscopic traffic flow model based on the kinematic wave theory, capable to repro-
duce the main traffic phenomena (hypocritical congestion, queues and spillback). The supply model is crucial for DTA, but
is not the focus of this paper; for details on how network performances are simulated in the proposed model refer to
Gentile (2015) . However, in the ICM it can be easily substituted with any alternative simulation model of traffic flows.
The demand model yields the network usage pattern in terms of destination flows q corresponding to the network
performance pattern in terms of travel times t for a given demand o-d matrix d :
q = Demand ( t ;d ) . (2)
Travel costs c of users are first obtained by combining the results of the traffic flow model (i.e. travel time, queue length,
etc.) with other relevant (flow-independent) attributes considered by drivers when making their route-choice (i.e. safety,
toll, etc.). Then, a sequential ( Gentile and Papola, 2006 ) Route Choice Model (RCM), deterministic or stochastic, allowing
for implicit path enumeration ( Gentile and Noekel, 2016 ) is applied to obtain the arc potentials w and the arc conditional
probabilities p , for each destination, like in Dial (1971) . Finally, a Flow Propagation Model (FPM) is applied to load travel
demand on the network, based on the arc conditional probabilities, thus obtaining the destination flows q . Both the RCM
and the Flow FPM require the travel times t , being this the main difference from static and dynamic assignment models.
The combination of the two sub-models yields a fixed point problem ( Bellei et al., 2005; Gentile et al., 2007 ) which
formalizes the user equilibrium, i.e. a traffic condition where no driver can improve his (perceived, in the case of stochastic
route choice model) path cost.

2.4. DTA model limitations and potential to represent rerouting phenomena
Neither DTA, nor DNL as formulated above are suitable to simulate unexpected events and in particular to model rerout-
ing phenomena. Indeed, in DTA the RCM assumes predictive information, and in DNL fixed demand pattern used in the
propagation model.
These are two extremal scenarios; typically part of the drivers behave accordingly with the DTA principle, and part with
the DNL. In the present paper we aim at dealing with such mixture of conditions.
Let us first consider the limit of DTA. In a dynamic RCM decisions are made based on future travel times taken from
previous experience as well as from traffic information and forecast. Implicitly, such predictive route choice paradigm implies
that a driver departing from his origin knows in advance the travel times that he will encounter until he arrives at his
destination: in the proposed model this is attained with Eq. (10) . Using the actual network characteristics including the
event to solve DTA results then in full knowledge about the future traffic states. Consequently, routing decisions would be
made to avoid the event before it occurs or becomes known, leading to unrealistic patterns.
Now, let us consider the limit of DNL. In the FPM the demand flows are loaded using routing decisions made pre-trip,
based on expected network performances not including the event. Thus, if the actual travel times happen to be different
from what was expected, only the propagation will be affected but no rerouting can be simulated.

===== PAGE 10 =====

R. Kucharski and G. Gentile / Transportation Research Part B 126 (2019) 414–441 423
The proposed ICM model was conceived to extend the above model structure of DTA to simulate rerouting phenomena.
Namely it exploits:
1. implicit path enumeration, where route choice probabilities are reproduced as splitting rates from any node to each
destination;
2. supply-demand separation, where actual travel times can be obtained for flows propagated with several independent
route choice models;
3. strong distinction between travel times and perceived travel costs, which allows to propagate flows along the path of
minimal perceived costs (possibly outdated) consistently with actual travel times;
4. flow conservation thanks to temporal layers formulation with propagation maps.
Thanks to these features, the ICM overcomes the above limits by ensuring that actual travel times cannot be perceived by
unaware drivers in their route choices, while all drivers, not just those rerouting, are propagated on the basis of the actual
travel times.
3. The Information Comply Model
Rerouting takes place when a driver tries to escape from some traffic event and en-trip decides to change path. We
assume the following rerouting behaviour:
1. a driver reroutes only once he becomes aware of the event, which may occur thanks to several information sources,
including direct observation;
2. the reason for rerouting is to avoid the negative consequences of the event and benefit from expected lower costs, which
shall then more than compensate his reluctance to change;
3. the driver reroutes to an optimal path considering the correct estimation of actual and future travel costs produced by
the event and by the rerouting of other drivers, based on experiences of similar situations and/or on predictive traffic
information.
This behaviour is reproduced in the ICM as a sequence of states and changes in the mental condition of a driver who:
• 1. is initially unaware about the event and follows his habitual path from his origin toward his destination, which has
been chosen pre-trip on the basis of the typical within-day evolution of travel times on the network in that day-type;
• 1–2. becomes informed about the event, while travelling on a certain arc of his habitual path, by some source of traffic
broadcast and/or by observing the current situation: he can use his own eyes to spot queues, or he can experience
anomalous travel times along his route;
• 2. is now aware of the event (and, by hypothesis, of all its consequences, including other drivers rerouting) but does not
see yet the necessity to reroute, although at every node he evaluates this alternative by properly considering the relative
advantage of changing path, by comparing the actual and typical travel times, as well as the stress of doing it;
• 2–3. decides to reroute at a certain node of his habitual path, when he reaches the conclusion that estimated delays are
so high that the situation deserves action to save a consistent amount of time;
• 3. follows the new optimal path until the destination, because his experience and/or available predictive information
allow him to make a correct forecast of the traffic that he will encounter from now on along the trip without major
surprises.
We have then assumed (and broadly discuss in Section 3.3 ) that:
• when the driver is informed, he perfectly becomes aware of the actual travel times, i.e. he is able to figure out the exact
nature of the event;
• experienced and/or informed drivers can predict, not only the direct consequences of the event (e.g. queues upstream of
the event and their spillback), but also that other drivers will reroute and generate congestion on alternative paths.
Clearly, the above strong assumptions represent a schematic simplification which is useful to model a complex phe-
nomenon like rerouting, whereas in reality knowledge of drivers is never perfect and information systems are never fully ac-
curate and predictive. However, these factors can be partially included in the proposed framework by considering a stochas-
tic route choice model (like the multinomial logit) rather than a deterministic one.
The demand flow in ICM is propagated in the three subsequent conditions: unaware, aware and rerouted ; with two tran-
sitions: information , i.e. becoming aware (ICM awareness model), and comply , i.e. deciding to reroute (ICM rerouting model).
We consider the user equilibrium results of DTA computed on the network without event as the typical traffic conditions,
denoted with a hat.
The immediate effects of the event can regard both the supply characteristics s (usually a temporary modification of
some arc capacity or free flow speed) and the dynamic o-d matrix d (usually an impulse in the generation or attraction of
some centroids). ICM allows to simulate one single event, which however can consist of a connected set of supply and/or
demand changes (e.g. some roads are closed for the Christmas market and the evening demand of the area is increased;
links are closed dynamically as a marathon moves through the city). As long as the information of all these changes spreads
jointly among drivers, the assumptions of the ICM hold fine. On the contrary, simulating a sequence of mostly independent

===== PAGE 11 =====

events (e.g. one accident on the motorway and another one inside the city an hour later) is impossible with the ICM and
requires, for example, a rolling horizon approach, like in Gentile et al. (2013) , who proposed a much simpler but more
flexible rerouting model.
By including these events in the DTA model and by replacing the classical demand sub-model with the above described
ICM (only in a qualitative way, so far), we obtain the actual traffic conditions, denoted without a hat. Therefore, typical
conditions are fixed and precomputed, while actual conditions are the result of a new user equilibrium with rerouting,
which will be found only when convergence of the proposed DTA method is attained.
3.1. The awareness model
The awareness model aims at representing how drivers become aware of the unexpected event. We use the term aware
to denote both being informed about the event and becoming conscious of experiencing atypical delays. The proposed model
shall include not only all the major sources of traffic information but also the educated guessing of experienced drivers.
As already signalized in the introduction, there are numerous ways the traffic information can be provided to the drivers.
For a review of fundamental ATIS strategies and information types see Khattak et al. (1993) . Here we consider and unify
three main information types: (a) generic (e.g. “tunnel blocked”), (b) prescriptive (e.g. “tunnel blocked, to reach City use
A4”), (c) personalized (e.g. “tunnel blocked, to reach destination follow this path”). We argue that they can be unified in one
cognitive paradigm for the rerouting driver. Namely, we assume that the aware driver (regardless what type of information
he received) will act as if he is aware of the actual network travel times and costs and their future evolution resulting from
the event and the drivers reaction to this event (rerouting), as further discussed in Section 3.3 .
Specifically, we assume that the most accurate forecast of travel costs is available at the level of Traffic Management
Centre (TMC) and broadcasted as a real-time route guidance, optimized VMS message, or online query made by drivers
curios of the reason for the experienced delay. If, however, the driver autonomously updates his expectations on travel costs,
it must be assumed that the driver is able to correctly predict the actual network costs. Under the above assumptions, we
unify the information provision types and assume that drivers become aware of the current and future network costs. This
allows to handle multiple information sources jointly and to propose a single behavioural model for rerouting (introduced in
Section 3.2 ). Though, including the consequences of other drivers rerouting in the prediction of the traffic pattern requires
to solve a circularity introduced in Section 3.3 .

3.1.4. Awareness through observation
Besides receiving information, drivers can become aware through observation. If an expert user of the road network
experiences a relevant delay wrt typical travel times while travelling along an arc of his habitual path, he may correctly
guess the reason and become aware about the event.
The motivation to assume this skilled behaviour is twofold. First of all, a driver is curious of the delay and may use
the available information sources to understand its cause, e.g. he can search on-line. Secondly, experienced drivers can
correctly estimate the network travel times by observing atypical travel times along their route ( Gao et al., 2008 ), especially
for recurrent but unexpected events (e.g. gridlocks in critical points of the network, or black-spots where accidents occur
frequently).

3.2. Rerouting model
The second step of ICM is aimed at representing the rerouting decision process of drivers who have already become
aware about the unexpected event and (as argued in Section 3.1 ) figured out the new cost pattern.
The proposed rerouting model will be evaluated repeatedly within the DTA algorithm (for each node, instant, destination,
and iteration). Because our requirement is the applicability of the method to large networks, the proposed model, apart from
being methodologically sound and realistic, needs to be light in terms of computation resources. Instead, the existing models
require relatively heavy calculation: correlation matrix ( Gao et al., 2008 ), path searches ( Pel et al., 2009 ), or agent within-day
replanning ( Dobler et al., 2012 ).
We propose a generic structure where the meaningful variables to express the utility of rerouting are provided at each
decision point. Here we use the simple binomial logit model; it can be however replaced with more complex Random Utility
Models. The model determines the discrete choice probability that aware drivers will reroute or not, subject to their current
location, their final destination and the traffic conditions that they expect. More specifically, the rerouting model yields the
share κ
i

path.
The two main reasons for which the aware drivers will reroute are:
• the availability of a better alternative which differs substantially from the typical path, i.e. to seek for gains;
• the estimate of significantly higher costs on the typical path wrt those on the currently optimal route choice, i.e. to avoid
losses.

and high expected gains and avoided losses are possible.

3.3. Rerouting path
In this step of ICM we reproduce how drivers choose a new path from the node where they decided to reroute to their
destination. Generally, drivers update their knowledge on the network performance pattern with the information they have
received and using their own prediction of the travel times resulting from the event.
The cognitive and decision process of predicting the future travel costs and reacting to them is not only complex, but also
nondeterministic, rarely rational, strongly dependent to the type of information and thus still remains an opened research
question (see, for example, Ramos (2015) and Selten et al. (2007) for more insights on how informed travellers predict
costs).
To formalize the model, we argue that drivers make rerouting decisions based on accurate information. We follow the
classic law of information provision by Bifulco et al. (2016) stating that the forecasted travel costs shall be in line with the
actually experienced ones. Yet, since complying to the information alters the costs that user was informed about, the fixed-
point problem arises. At the convergence of this fixed-point problem, rerouting drivers utilize the information on forecasted
evolution of the system including anticipated reaction of drivers to that information. Accordingly, the rerouting drivers will
experience the rerouting costs they were informed about. We propose the ICM model, where results are obtained after
solving a fixed-point problem between information and drivers reaction to that information (we illustrate the convergence
and intermediate results in the Sioux-Falls example in Section 5.2 ).
Thanks to such approach, ICM model can be formalized with assumption that rerouting drivers use the arc conditional
probabilities, p = RCM ( c,t ), computed with the RCM based on the actual costs and times. Clearly, at each iteration the costs
may change since the rerouting flows will yield different costs. Likewise, the arc probabilities used for rerouting will change,
until finally reaching convergence of this particular type of equilibrium (not fully in line with Wardrop principles though),
where not all drivers are perfectly informed and comply with a rational behaviour.
While the above assumptions may seem unrealistic and doubtful, they allow to keep the model deterministic and formal.
Assuming that the rerouting drivers do not anticipate others retouring decisions at all would be even more unrealistic. As-
suming partial (incomplete) anticipation would be arbitrary and intractable. Assuming perfect anticipation allows to obtain
a formal model without heuristics, which allows for scenario comparison and benchmarking.
Notably, proposed model does necessarily assume exceptional drivers predictive capabilities, since the information may
be externally provided. Moreover, we believe that for rerouting phenomena, the effect of information spread and compliance
is much more significant than the path choice process when the rerouting decision is made. Critical is when and where the
driver receives the information rather than the assumptions on rerouting path choice behaviour, which do not change the
essence of rerouting picture resulting from ICM.
Also, the route choice model selection is also of high importance. Particular attention is to be paid for deterministic
models, whose choice map is one-to-many. In this case, if all-or nothing assignment is considered in the demand model,
small changes of costs will yield substantial changes in arc conditional probabilities. This can be avoided by considering at
every iteration of the equilibrium method the probabilities provided by gradient projection algorithms, as in Gentile (2016) ,
which depend smoothly on arc performances. In the numerical experiments we mainly utilize deterministic route-choice
model not to make confusion between the stochasticity of the route choice model and that of the information and comply
process. Yet if the non-deterministic driver behaviour needs to be reproduced, stochastic model can be used as shown in
one of numerical tests. Importantly, the rerouting route-choice model can be differently parameterized than the typical RCM
to capture more stochastic nature of rerouting choices, different utility formulation etc.

6. Summary and conclusions
The paper’s objective was to investigate DTA capabilities of modelling consequences of unexpected events and to propose
its modification allowing to represent rerouting phenomena. Based on DTA structure analysis we proposed Information Com-
ply Model and integrated it into macroscopic DTA model of Gentile (2016) . ICM originates from the thorough definition of

===== PAGE 27 =====

440 R. Kucharski and G. Gentile / Transportation Research Part B 126 (2019) 414–441
the drivers behaviour and their decision process. Dividing the decision process into two steps: becoming aware and deciding
to reroute, allows handling two processes separately. Thanks to this multiple information sources available in contemporary
traffic networks can be handled consistently. Impact of VMS, online sources, radio broadcasts can be handled jointly and
further extended with drivers guessing about atypical situation from an atypical delay. Thanks to this, number of aware
drivers (knowing about the event) can be consistently estimated as a function of location and time – which is crucial, as
informing process can strongly depend on time (for the radio news) and location (for VMS). In our model only aware drivers
may reroute, but not all aware drivers will reroute. Rerouting decision is driven by the rerouting utility, composed of two
factors: avoiding losses and maximizing gains efficiently computed from the cost differences between typical and actual sit-
uations and further used in binomial logit to estimate rerouting probability in a given location and time. Thanks to this we
can capture the rerouting only in cases when costs changed substantially and only at nodes with efficient alternatives. ICM
is integrated in the modified demand model of DTA which now handles three drivers conditions of flow: unaware, aware
and rerouted. Transitions between the conditions are handled with the awareness and rerouting models at each node of the
propagation.
Final contribution of the paper, ICM-DTA, extends the DTA by calculating the network performance and traffic flows in
cases of unexpected events. Proposed model overcomes the limitations of classic DTA methods (perfect information and pre-
dictive behaviour). It allows to efficiently compute travel times and vehicle flows for the unexpected event with acceptable
computation burden. ICM can be computed in a standard DTA environment with input that is available and it provides a
valuable, meaningful results in-line with what can be observed. In the case-study we demonstrated that the main objective
was achieved (the rerouting phenomena was reproduced and computation times remained acceptable). In the parallel paper
( Kucharski and Gentile, 2015 ) we show how ICM can be estimated with the field data to represent the actual phenom-
ena, while in on-going research our aim is to use GPS od trajectories to understand the rerouting behaviour and calibrate
rerouting model.
There are number of directions to pursue in context of this research. Starting from the awareness model, where more
source types could be included and supported with empirical findings. Further research on how the data spreads online can
bring a valuable insight, as well as testing if the awareness sources are correlated. Various types of information (raw fact,
forecast, guidance, travel time, etc.) shall be included in the spreading process with various impact on the behaviour. The
proposed model deals with a fixed od demand, while events affect whole activity plans which can change due to events,
thus an extension to event-depended od demand as defined in Dobler et al. (2012) might be a good improvement.
Also, the drivers’ behaviour is probably more heterogenic and the single RCM for all users may be not valid, especially in
unexpected cases. To this end one can introduce multiple demand classes, or use the perceived costs (derived from actual
costs) for rerouting path. Similarly, one can substitute logit model with alternative discrete choice-model to overcome the
issues of applying it sequentially along the trajectory, which in current version can lead to intractable results. The promising
solutions seem to be: a) calculating rerouting utility up to the destination and determining the rerouting probability once,
b) introducing a ‘memory’ into the sequential model by memorizing share of drivers that did not reroute and the utility that
they rejected, c) discrete-choice models which can be applied sequentially (like recursive logit of Fosgerau et al. (2013) ).
Finally, the biggest need for rerouting models is probably in real-time environment (TMC), yet to make ICM work in
real-time, e.g. in rolling-horizon ( Gentile et al., 2013 ) the awareness and rerouting models should be redefined for multiple
independent events with independently computed awareness, rerouting utility and rerouting path.
