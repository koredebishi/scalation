1. Introduction
Models are just approximations of reality, and car-following (CF) models are no exception. Thus, we will never have a CF model
delivering a zero error, whatever goodness-of-fit function (GoF) and measure of performance (MoP), or combination of MoPs, are used.
This simple fact has profound implications on the calibration of CF dynamics.
As models are imperfect – and only because models are imperfect – it is not the same if we calibrate them on a different MoP and/or
a different GoF. Consequentially, CF model calibration is prone to ambiguities, confusions, and even contradictions, as unfortunately
confirmed by our state-of-the-art review, despite the fact that CF modelling is unarguably one of the oldest and most frequently studied
topics in traffic flow theory. These problems lead to a subjectivity of CF modelling research and impedes its fast progress. Particularly,
the wide spectrum of calibration settings in the literature makes it impossible to quantitatively compare and sift results from different
studies using different models and datasets (e.g., exposing the bad while retaining the good), and hence undermines the transparency

This paper aims at changing this trend by providing a sound recommendation on calibration settings based on a vast experimental
analysis and on previous theoretical results.
Furthermore, the results of this study are reproducible. Methodology, codes and data underlying the study and its recommenda-
tions, are shared with the scientific community. Thus, future developments – being them a new or different GoF, a new model or a new
trajectory dataset – could be simply plugged-in in the study framework and compared in a fair and impartial manner.
To inform the experimental analysis in this study, a comprehensive review of how CF dynamics have been calibrated in the recent
literature has been carried out. A state of confusion, contradictions, and even malpractices emerged (Sections 2 and 3). We have then
tested and ranked all the combinations of MoPs and GoFs used in the literature so far (Section 4). To this aim four different well-known
car-following models/adaptive cruise control algorithms (ACC) have been calibrated on four trajectory datasets, including car-
following dynamics of both human-driven vehicles and automated vehicles (i.e. vehicles equipped with ACC).
Grounded on this experimental analysis and on previous theoretical results (see Punzo and Montanino, 2016), two types of
recommendation are provided in the study (Section 5).
First, a number of calibration settings, which are inappropriate but often used in the field literature, have been identified. In the
epistemological framework we assume in this study, this result is deemed to be definitive. One sound refutation is sufficient to dismiss a
general theory, indeed (see the essay by Popper, 1963, on conjectures and refutations). All the more so, if a calibration setting appears
inappropriate in the vast majority of experimental scenarios (i.e. with different car-following models and datasets), it cannot have
general validity. For instance, hereafter we will report that RMSE(v) is worse than RMSE(s) in 93.5% of cases. Therefore, in a future
experiment, one should avoid using a setting that has repeatedly failed in the literature. Although there may exist some probability that
such a setting worked in a specific experimental scenario, it would remain to be demonstrated that it was the most efficient in that
scenario (see the next paragraph).
Then a sound recommendation about what MoP and GoF should be used for CF dynamics calibration is given. Unlike the first
recommendation, we are aware that this one is not definitive, since experimental results, though wide, will never be a proof. There may
be other experiments carried out on different models or data, or simply proposing new calibration settings that may provide different
or better results than those in this study. However, these new results would at most impact the optimality, but not the soundness of the
recommendation in this study. Furthermore, the superiority of any new result can only be established by comparing it with previous
efficient settings (like the recommended settings in this study) in an impartial and reproducible manner e.g., through the methodo-
logical framework (and the codes) provided here.
Although the main focus of the paper is on calibration settings, the experimental framework also allowed interesting comparisons
of models and trajectory datasets of automated and human-driven vehicles to be made (see Section 5).
What’s more, this paper is reproducible, and anyone who is interested in checking any part of it can generate the same content in
the open source RStudio (RStudio, 2020).

Remark 2. When either one or more measurements are available (e.g. only spacing from GPS, or spacing from GPS and relative speed
from radar), the integration/differentiation scheme of data needs to be the same adopted in model simulation. Otherwise, the residuals
given by f(MoPobs,MoPsim) would be caused by the CF model approximation, as well as by a different integration/differentiation
scheme between model and data (for integration schemes in CF models, see Treiber and Kesting, 2013; and Treiber and Kanagaraj,
2015. For a thorough introduction to errors and uncertainty in traffic simulation, see Punzo and Montanino, 2020).
Regarding optimization algorithms, a systematic and unbiased assessment of different algorithms (and calibration settings) was
performed in Punzo et al. (2012), in a laboratory experiment. Synthetic trajectories were generated through a CF model, by assuming a
given set of model parameter values. Model calibrations were run with the objective of rediscovering these pre-determined parameter
values. Only genetic algorithm (GA) and combined scatter-search and gradient-based NLP solver (LINDO OptQuest) were able to
perform satisfactorily. Downhill simplex and other algorithms, which were not reported in that paper for the sake of brevity (i.e.
Interior Point, Trust Region Reflective and Sequential Quadratic Programming solvers), failed to rediscover the predetermined
parameter values. As claimed in the Introduction, we consider the proved inability of some algorithms to successfully calibrate a CF
model – in a systematic and fair test environment – as a sufficient proof to dismiss these algorithms. Meanwhile, the good performances
of GA and OptQuest suggest that choosing them as a general optimization tool for CF calibration is reasonable, though not necessarily
optimal or valid in every circumstance, e.g., for CF calibration against macroscopic traffic data such as loop detector data or average
travel times (e.g. Brockfeld et al., 2003; Ciuffo et al., 2008; Cascetta et al., 2011). For a review of optimization algorithms used in CF
model calibration see Rafati Fard and Shariat Mohaymany (2019).

2.2. Comments about the literature review
Our comprehensive literature review reveals three unsettling points:
• out of 994 articles we have collected and scrutinized from the target journals since 2005, only a small fraction (9%) attempted to
calibrate CF models. Among these articles:
• an array of MoPs was used:
– 41% calibrated on spacing; 34% on speed, 13% on acceleration, 9% on spacing and speed, and 3% on speed standard deviation.
– 91% calibrated on a single MoP, and 9% on multiple MoPs.
̃
• an array of GoFs was used: RMSE (42%), RMSPE (14%), Theil’s U (20%), MAE (9%), MAPE (2%), RMSPE (7%), and NRMSE (6%).
The wide spectrum of calibration settings used in these recent studies, and reported in Table 1 and 2, indicates that the state-of-the-
art of CF model calibration has been plagued with confusion and contradictions. Even worse, many of these calibration settings are
wrong, as they are not structurally able to reproduce the observed car-following dynamics (see Punzo et al. 2012).
Sometimes a CF model is calibrated on a traffic characteristic that is a nonlinear transformation of basic CF model outputs, i.e. of
spacing, speed and acceleration (e.g., Tian et al., 2016). Calibrating a model on a nonlinear transformation of an output, e.g. the speed
standard deviation, means calibrating a nonlinear transformation of the model itself. As a consequence, when a model is calibrated on
such a measure transformation, though it will reproduce the intended characteristic at its best, resulting calibrated CF dynamics will be
non-optimal.
Remark 4. The only MoPs that shall be applied in the CF model calibration problem in Eq. (1) are s, v and a, or their linear
combinations.
Leaving aside unsuitable or sub-optimal calibration settings, the wide spectrum of settings emerging from the literature is in itself a
hindrance to scientific advancements in the field. In fact, it undermines transparency and reproducibility, making it impossible to
quantitatively compare results from different studies. In contrast, had all these studies adopted the same calibration setting, it would be
possible to compare different models from studies using the same data, or compare performances of a model against different datasets.
By doing so, weak or powerful models, and corresponding developments, would be easily identified and distinguished, which would
enable researchers to easily reproduce results reported by other researchers, and choose the most promising research path while
abandoning the misleading ones. Actually, the benefits of having the same calibration setting appear even before a study is published.
At the paper review stage, the same calibration setting can help in achieving a fairer and impartial evaluation of studies, while saving
the authors’ and reviewers’ time.
In the next two sections, therefore, we will present theoretical and experimental bases to draw a sound guideline on what cali-
bration settings shall or shall not be used.
3. The solved case of MoP
Looking at Table 1, one may conclude that there is still no consensus on what MoP should be used in a CF model calibration setting.
In truth, this long-standing methodological problem in the car-following theory was solved in a recent paper, Punzo and Montanino
(2016).

Depending on the MoP used in calibration, the resulting set of optimal parameter values for a CF model will be different (again,
since we do not have a perfect CF model). If we calibrate on speed, we will reach optimality on speed, but resulting spacing will be non-
optimal (optimality on spacing is reached when calibrating on spacing, of course). In other words, we have contrasting optimization
objectives, which inevitably leads to the fact that the optimality on one MoP is detrimental to the optimality on another MoP. Thus, if
we calibrate on speed, we cannot reduce the error on spacing without at the same time worsening the error on speed, and vice versa.
This property is also known as Pareto-efficiency and the two optimal solutions are both Pareto-efficient (Iancu and Trichakis, 2014).
Notwithstanding, the two optimal solutions are not equally desirable from a modelling point of view. In fact, we can quantify the
degradation of one measure when the model is calibrated on the other, and vice versa. Between the two, preference will go to the
measure producing the least error in the other measure, relative to the minimum obtainable. In fact, the modeler’s objective is to obtain
a model which is able to consistently reproduce both speed and spacing.
In Punzo and Montanino (2016), a sound physical interpretation of this issue was provided based on mathematical developments, i.
e. why a calibration on spacing generally yields a relative error on speed which is smaller than the error on spacing caused by a
calibration on speed.
The interpretation is based on Eq. (2) and Eq. (3). In the equations, specifications of the objective functions are given for a model
calibration on speed and spacing, respectively (the adopted GoF is the Sum of Squared Errors, SSE):

4.1. Methodology
As discussed before, CF model calibration is a multi-objective optimization problem. Solutions β* to this problem, also known as
Pareto-efficient, yields objective points z=f(β*)located on the so-called Pareto frontier. A solution yielding an objective point on the
frontier is also called non-dominated, since it is not possible to find any other solution performing better in all objectives (see Fig. 1).
As mentioned in Section 3, not all Pareto-efficient solutions are equivalent. Usually in micro-economics, “indifference curves” are
introduced to compare solutions. In the objectives space f(β)⊆Rn, each curve is the locus of the objective points f(β)that share the same
utility for a consumer.
In our case, if we assume that all objectives have the same importance for the analyst (i.e. errors in spacing, speed and acceleration
are equally weighed), an indifference curve is given by all objective points having the same distance from the so-called utopic point z*.
This point is the ideal objective point obtained by adopting the minimum achievable error for each objective. In case of two objectives/
MoPs, e.g., speed and spacing, an indifference curve is any circle in the objectives plane centred in the utopic point; in case of three
objectives, i.e., s, v, a, we will have a sphere.
All that said, the most preferable solution among the Pareto-efficient ones is the solution whose objective point lies on the
indifference curve closest to the utopic point. That is, the Pareto-optimal solution β* whose objective point f(β*)has the minimum
distance from the utopic point among all other Pareto-optimal solutions. See Fig. 1.
In the figure, the utopic point coincides with the axes origin since each objective is a relative error i.e., normalized over the
minimum. In general, in the calibration of a CF model, if we assume to measure the error on each objective “i” by the RMSE, i.e.
f(β)=RMSE(F(β)), the coordinates of the utopic point, z*, are:

Concerning the comparison of objective functions, several observations from the results in the tables are worth noting:
• When a single MoP is applied:
– Regardless of the GoF, the model and the dataset, spacing is preferable as MoP. In fact, inequality in Eq. (7), on average (i.e., see
Eq. (13)), and the equivalent one between speed and acceleration hold for every GoF, model and dataset. This is a further
experimental confirmation of the results in Punzo and Montanino (2016).
– Focusing on spacing, regardless of the model and dataset, GoFs which are not based on percentage errors i.e., RMSE(s), Theil’s U
(s) and MAE(s) are always more preferable than percentage-based GoFs i.e., RMSPE and MAPE (with the only exception of the
IDM/Hefei scenario, shown in Appendix C).
• When multiple MoPs are applied:
– NRMSE(s, v, a) and Theil’s U(s, v, a) are the two most preferable Pareto-efficient objective functions in the vast majority of the
calibration scenarios. NRMSE is the most robust between the two, since in the ACC dataset for the FVDM-CTH, U(s, v, a) is one of
the worst performing objective functions among those containing s as MoP.
– Percentage-based objective functions, i.e., RMSPE and MAPE are less preferable than non-percentage-based ones, i.e., NRMSE
and Theil’s U, either with two MoPs, i.e., s, v (with the only exception of the IDM/Hefei scenario), or three MoPs, i.e., s, v, a. In
addition, in many calibration experiments, percentage-based objective functions did not return Pareto-efficient solutions (in
general, percentage-based objective functions are not recommended, since they require thresholds on speed and acceleration,
and overweigh lower value measurements in calibration).
It is also worth noting that NRMSE(s, v, a) and Theil’s U(s, v, a) have never been used in the literature, to the best of our knowledge.
NRMSE was used with a single MoP, i.e. spacing or speed (see e.g., Kesting and Treiber, 2008; Papathanasopoulou and Antoniou, 2015)
but, in such a case, it is easy to show that NRMSE is equivalent to RMSE or SSE.
Results in Tables 3 and 4 (plus those in Appendix C) are also useful to compare the model performances, across these different
trajectory datasets.
Gipps’ model and the IDM turned out to be the best-performing models on human-driven vehicle datasets. In particular, Gipps’
model has the lowest error on spacing across all datasets (speed and acceleration errors are comparable to the minimum calibration
errors, which are achieved by the IDM). Focusing on the automated vehicles dataset, Gipps’ model turned out to have the lowest
calibration error on spacing and speed, among the four tested models (the acceleration error is very close to the minimum value, which
is achieved by the IDM). It is somewhat surprising that Gipps’ model, which is based on an idealized safe human driving behaviour, has
been the best model to approximate automated vehicle control logics, outperforming controllers which are customarily applied to
simulate ACC vehicles (i.e., FVDM with CTH and SIGMOID spacing policies).
Variances of relative calibration errors across the population of vehicles in Naples’ dataset, are visualised using box plots in Figs. 6
and 7, as an example. As shown in these box plots, the variances of the relative calibration errors are significantly different across
different objective functions. In general, the objective functions that are based either on speed only, or on acceleration only, display
very large variances in their relative errors on spacing. Conversely, the objective functions based on spacing are much more robust,
showing small variances of relative errors on speed as well as on acceleration. NRMSE(s,v,a) and U(s,v,a) consistently show small
relative calibration errors on all three measures.

5. A guideline on calibration settings
In light of previous results and in the spirit of the present study, we provide two qualitatively different recommendations.
The first one concerns those calibration settings which are clearly inadequate, and as such should be avoided. This recommendation
is deemed to be definitive according to the epistemological assumptions of this study (see the Introduction).
The second recommendation concerns the calibration settings which have shown to be the most preferable among the Pareto-
efficient ones. Despite the fact that the study spans over four different models and four different datasets, this recommendation
cannot be claimed to be final, since there might be other calibration settings that are not considered here but would perform better than
those recommended (in these scenarios and in others).
1) Unsuitable settings in CF model calibration:
• If one single MoP is adopted in the objective function, speed and acceleration should be avoided. Spacing should be used instead, as it
is more preferable (see Eq. (7)).
• If two or more MoPs are adopted in the objective function, percentage-based GoFs (i.e., RMSPE and MAPE) should be avoided,
since either they are not Pareto-efficient or they are less preferable than non-percentage-based objective functions like NRMSE
and Theil’s U.
2) Pareto-efficient settings for CF model calibration:
• NRMSE(s,v,a) and Theil’s U(s,v,a) are the most preferable objective functions among those being Pareto-efficient.
• Since both NRMSE(⋅) and Theil’s U(⋅) have similar performances whether two or three MoPs are considered (i.e. s,v or s,v,a), if
acceleration data are noisy (which often is the case in practice), NRMSE(s,v) or Theil’s U(s,v) is preferred.
Based on results about parameter distributions (see Fig. 8 concerning the time headway), we hold a certain preference for NRMSE
(⋅) over Theil’s U(⋅).
Note that a proper acquisition and treatment of data are preliminary to the identification of unsuitable and suitable calibration
settings. The following three prerequisites need to be guaranteed (see Section 2 and Remarks 1 and 2):
• when pre-processing trajectory data, care must be taken to avoid error amplification in the differentiation process (noise), and error
accumulation in the integration process (bias);
• if several measures are independently gathered through separate devices, these measurements need to be (made) “internally
consistent”;
• the integration/differentiation scheme of data needs to be the same as the one adopted in the model simulation.
6. Implications on research transparency and reproducibility
For traffic flow research in general, and CF modelling in particular, a widely agreed CF model calibration setting is a prerequisite for
transparency and reproducibility of research. Without adopting the same setting in different studies, it would be impossible to
quantitatively and objectively compare different CF models, eventually calibrated on different datasets. By adopting the same setting
researchers would be helped in choosing the most promising research paths while abandoning the misleading ones, which could also
save reviewers’ time.

7. Concluding remarks
We are at a crossroads where it has to be decided how academic work should be conducted, documented and disseminated. The
belief that reproducible research is an unstoppable and inevitable future has been gaining a massive following in many disciplines (e.g.,
biostatistics, signal processing, statistical analysis, etc.). Still, it is not widespread in the Transportation Research community.
By providing clear guidelines for calibrating car-following models, and making this paper totally reproducible, it is the authors’
hope that it could not only end the state of confusion and contradictions that has plagued the CF modelling literature for so long, but
also inspire the start of a wide practice of transparent and reproducible research in CF dynamics modelling. A spark that could
hopefully spread into other branches of Transportation Research.
In order to provide a guideline on calibration setting we have made an extensive calibration exercise by comparing all the objective
functions adopted in the field literature so far, and some new ones. The analysis has been based on four models of car-following
dynamics – widely adopted in the fields of both traffic flow theory and control theory – and on four different datasets, including
trajectories of human-driven and automated vehicles.
A methodology to compare and rank objective functions in terms of Pareto-efficiency has been developed. The criterion proposed,
in particular, gives preference to the objective function providing the least distance from the utopic point. In order to compare results
from objective functions with different units of measurement, a relative error with respect to the minimum achievable error over each
objective has been proposed.
Based on the analysis of the extensive calibration experiments carried out, two qualitatively different recommendations have been
provided.
The first one discourages the use of the objective functions which have consistently shown to be either not Pareto-efficient, or not
preferable according to the proposed criterion across all the models and datasets. This recommendation is deemed to be final based on
the epistemological assumptions of this study. In fact, these objective functions have no general validity. If one still intends to use them
in a specific case, their scenario-specific superiority must be demonstrated against all other objective functions, before use.
The second recommendation is about calibration settings which have shown to be the most preferable according to the proposed
criterion, among the Pareto-efficient ones. Despite the fact that the study spans over four different models and four different and
diverse datasets, this recommendation cannot be claimed to be final, since there might be other calibration settings, which are not
considered here, that could perform better than those recommended. However, aside from new objective functions, an upheaval of the
provided ranking is not expected, given the robustness of presented results across the quite wide range of models and datasets used in
this study.
Any different model, calibration setting, or trajectory dataset could be plugged-in in the computer codes that we share with the
community. Thus, it could be tested in a fair and impartial manner against models, settings and trajectories applied in this study or
added by other researchers.
